# Social strategy and production decisions

Start with the exact approved client foundation and current paid service scope. Use audience, offer, voice, available assets and supplied performance evidence; do not recreate brand identity inside a caption task. A useful content matrix connects **customer need → message → pillar → format → visual scene → source → next action → owner**. Cadence follows scope and production capacity rather than a repository's generic frequency table.

Channel adaptation changes structure, not just length. Instagram needs the visual story and the transition from feed/Reel/Story/Highlight to enquiry; LinkedIn needs professional relevance and a clear person-versus-company voice; Facebook needs page/community context and accurate local service facts. Build each carousel page around a different communication job and primary scene. The opening promise must be fulfilled by the sequence. Give captions and asset briefs useful complete content, not instructions for somebody else to invent it.

Use actual Insights exports to compare like periods and formats. Define reach/impressions/views, engagement denominator, paid versus organic contribution and missing fields. Keep total counts separate from rates, use an explicit timezone and avoid division by zero. A calendar is proposed until the connected account/publishing route returns a saved scheduling receipt. A timing suggestion or available social account is not proof a post is queued.

Every production row needs exact account/client, final asset revision, copy, accessibility text, format/crop, proposed date/timezone, dependencies and approval state. If evidence is missing, identify the smallest requested input. Candidate learning: describe the defect, its source/output versions, repair and the limited clients/channels for which the rule is useful; approved retrieval is handled by the coordinator.

## Carousel calibration — fictional example

For a five-page carousel promised as “How a reusable cup cuts daily friction”, use: (1) commuter problem/scene and hook; (2) one verified product feature shown close up; (3) a realistic use sequence; (4) cleaning/storage detail supported by supplied facts; (5) audience-specific next step. Do not invent capacity, heat retention or dishwasher safety. Each scene does a different job. If the brief is a single caption, deliver the caption rather than this full planning structure.

A failed opening such as “Five reasons this cup saves money” with no cost evidence should become a supported promise or request the needed price/use assumptions. Do not keep the unsupported claim merely because later pages look good.
